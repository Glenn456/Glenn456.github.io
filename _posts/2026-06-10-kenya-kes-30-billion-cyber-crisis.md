## When the Attack Surface Is the Economy

Most countries treat cybersecurity as an IT problem. Kenya cannot afford that framing.

Mobile money transactions in Kenya account for more than 53% of GDP. The East African region hosts 459 million mobile money accounts. M-PESA alone processes roughly KES 50 billion in transactions annually. When the payment infrastructure is the economy, a successful cyberattack is not a technical outage. It is a macroeconomic event.

That framing matters because the numbers coming out of Kenya's regulators are not just cybersecurity statistics. They are economic indicators.

---

## The Numbers

Between April and June 2025, the Communications Authority of Kenya's National KE-CIRT/CC recorded over **4.5 billion cyber threat events** in a single quarter. During the same period, an estimated **KES 29.9 billion (approximately USD 230 million)** was lost to cybercrime.

In the following quarter, July to September 2025, regulators recorded a further **842 million threat events**, described largely as automated scanning and hostile probing of Kenya's digital infrastructure. Malware-related incidents during the period rose to **103 million**, up from 99 million in the comparable window a year prior.

Mobile banking fraud cases surged 87% in the most recent reporting period, driven by social engineering, credential compromise, and SIM-swap schemes.

For context: these are not unique, targeted attacks by sophisticated adversaries. Much of the volume is automated. Scanning tools, credential stuffing scripts, and phishing infrastructure running around the clock against a digital economy that has scaled faster than its security controls.

---

## Why Kenya Is a Target

The answer is in the infrastructure.

Kenya built one of the world's most successful digital payment ecosystems at speed. M-PESA went from a pilot to national infrastructure in years, not decades. That speed created a payment layer that works remarkably well and a security layer that has consistently lagged behind.

The attack surface that resulted has three distinct layers that attackers exploit:

**The customer layer.** Nearly half of Kenyan mobile users report receiving fraudulent banking messages designed to steal login credentials. Social engineering works here because users trust their phones. In a country where M-PESA is how you pay rent, buy food, and receive salary, the mobile number is not just a communication tool. It is a financial identity.

**The institutional layer.** More than 90% of financial institutions have reported at least one cyberattack in the past year, ranging from malware attacks and phishing scams to breaches targeting sensitive customer data. The Central Bank of Kenya has identified cyber threats as the top challenge to digital banking innovation. The sector is under sustained, continuous pressure.

**The infrastructure layer.** Kenya Power, Kenya Railways Corporation, and the National Transport and Safety Authority have all experienced attacks affecting their digital systems. Critical infrastructure is not separate from the financial attack surface. It is part of it.

## The Third-Party Risk Nobody Is Talking About

One of the most underreported dimensions of Kenya's cyber exposure is third-party and vendor risk.

A reported breach involving compromised contractors cost a Kenyan bank over KSh 517 million, highlighting weaknesses in third-party access control.

This is the pattern that dominated global cybersecurity in 2025 and 2026: attackers do not breach the institution. They breach a vendor, a contractor, or a middleware provider that has access to the institution's systems, and walk in through a trusted channel.

In Kenya's fintech stack, this risk is structural. Banks, fintechs, and mobile network operators are deeply integrated through APIs, middleware layers, and shared payment rails. A vulnerability in any one layer creates exposure for every institution connected to it.

The 2020 breach at Uganda's Pegasus Technologies illustrated this precisely. Attackers exploited middleware linking banks to mobile wallets and used approximately 2,000 SIM cards to siphon roughly USD 3 million. The vulnerability was not in the bank's core systems. It was in the API layer connecting them to the mobile money ecosystem.

## The Execution Gap

74% of organisations in East Africa rank cyber risk as a top strategic concern, yet only 29% conduct regular tabletop exercises to simulate crises. A cybersecurity firm's 2026 report described this as an "execution gap" between boardroom anxiety and operational readiness.

The gap is visible in specific ways:

Banks are aware of ransomware but many are still running outdated software that has known vulnerabilities. Regulators are issuing advisories but enforcement remains inconsistent. KE-CIRT/CC issued 19.9 million cyber threat advisories in a single quarter, an extraordinary volume that suggests the advisory model is not translating into action at the pace required.

The skills gap compounds this. Experienced cybersecurity professionals are difficult to find and expensive to retain in Kenya. This is not unique to Kenya, it is a global problem, but it is acutely felt in a market where the demand for security talent has outpaced the supply of training programmes that can produce it.

---

## What Needs to Change

In Kenya, where digital payments, national identity systems, and public services operate at population scale, a cyber failure is more than a technical disruption. It is a macroeconomic risk.

The response to that risk needs to match its scale:

**Move from compliance-driven to threat-informed security.** Most institutions are building security programmes around regulatory checklists. Threat-informed security starts with understanding who is actually attacking you, with what techniques, and where your specific environment has gaps.

**Invest in detection, not just prevention.** The volume of automated threats means some will always get through. The question is how quickly you detect and contain them. Institutions that still rely on traditional perimeter tools and have no meaningful SOC capability are building defences against the threat model of a decade ago.

**Treat the API layer as a security boundary.** Every integration between a bank and a fintech, between a mobile operator and a payment platform, between a lender and a credit bureau, is a potential attack vector. API security is not a developer concern. It is a security architecture requirement.

**Build sector-wide threat intelligence sharing.** Attackers share tools, techniques, and target lists. Kenyan financial institutions largely do not share threat intelligence with each other. A Financial Sector Computer Security Incident Response Team (FS-CSIRT) with real-time information sharing would change the asymmetry.

---

## The M-PESA CISO Said It Best

Tim Theuri, CISO at M-PESA, put the stakes clearly: "For SMEs and households, any interruption can translate into real hardship. Once trust is lost, people retreat to cash. Ultimate success is when you find a grandmother in Africa with no fear, trusting her hard-earned cash in a digital system."

That grandmother represents fifty years of financial inclusion progress. She also represents the target that every credential thief, SIM swapper, and ransomware operator is ultimately attacking.

Protecting her is not just a technical problem. It is an economic imperative.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kenya recorded 4.5 billion threat events in a single quarter of 2025, with losses of KES 29.9 billion during the same period.
- Mobile banking fraud surged 87%, driven primarily by SIM swap attacks, credential theft, and social engineering.
- Third-party and API layer vulnerabilities are among the most underaddressed risks in Kenya's fintech stack.
- 74% of East African organisations rank cyber risk as a top concern, but only 29% conduct crisis simulation exercises.
- The execution gap between awareness and operational readiness is the defining security challenge for Kenyan financial institutions in 2026.
